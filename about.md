I still remember the specific feeling the first time I encountered a command line in my second year of graduate school. I had just made the decision to transition into computational biology, and what I felt looking at that blinking cursor was not curiosity — it was a quiet, overwhelming dread. Nothing about it looked like science. It looked like a wall.

Years later, having gone further down that road, I find myself thinking about that moment often. Not because the tools got easier — they did, eventually — but because I've come to understand that the wall wasn't really about the command line at all. It was about not yet knowing what question I was trying to answer with it.

> Research should begin with a question. Running a pipeline well requires a clear understanding of the biological problem it is meant to answer, not just the code that executes it.

A conversation with a friend brought this into sharper focus for me recently. We were talking, somewhat casually, about how difficult it would be to catch errors in AI-generated analysis — how you would even know if the code or methodology that a tool like ChatGPT or Claude suggested was actually correct for your specific biological question. A few weeks later, a graduate student asked me almost the same thing directly: *"How do I know if the answer or code an AI gives me is right for this analysis?"*

The question stayed with me. And I think the honest answer is: it is genuinely difficult to identify errors in AI output when you haven't first run those analyses yourself, or seen what the results are supposed to look like, or understood which methodological choices are appropriate for which questions.

The better approach, I've come to believe, is to **start with the question and not the tool**. A solid understanding of the biological problem you are trying to solve is the single most reliable mechanism for catching errors — whether those errors come from an AI assistant, a published pipeline, or your own code. If you know what the analysis is supposed to do and why, you will notice when the output doesn't make biological sense.

## What this blog does

**Thinking in Genomes** is a question-driven blog about genomics, evolution, and metagenomics. Every entry begins with a biological question — the kind a researcher or student might actually be trying to answer. The biological reasoning comes first. Then the analysis: the appropriate class of method, the tools involved, and the choices that matter. Then interpretation — what the results actually tell you, and what they don't.

There are already excellent tutorials out there that walk through bioinformatics pipelines step by step. This blog starts with the question. The aim is to be the thing you read *before* those tutorials: the framing that helps you understand why you are running a particular analysis, so that when you do — or when you ask an AI to help you — you are in a position to think critically about what comes back.

I am still learning. Much of what I write here reflects things I am working through myself. What I am certain of is the core idea: the researchers who get the most out of computational tools are the ones who can reason clearly about the biology first. That is what I mean by thinking in genomes.

*I hope something here is useful to you, wherever you are in that journey.*
